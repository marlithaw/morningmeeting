Teach Reset and Return

Monday through Thursday, each grade band learns one part of the classroom response process: what gets in the way of learning (action and impact), the Level 1 steps, the written reflection, and Level 2 support with the return to learning. The language on the slides matches the From Lap to Tap process, the Reset and Return written reflections, and the Two-Minute Return, so students hear the same words from every adult. Friday’s Morning Spark (Friday Wonder) carries the same theme, followed by Friday Cypher.

Timing: Welcome ready screen; Regulate 2, Orient 2, Connect 3 (Look 2 and Share 1), Practice 3, Commit 3, Transition 2 minutes.

Respect and privacy, every day: use only the fictional students and situations on the slides. Students never describe a real classmate’s behavior, a time a classmate was removed, or a visit to the Restorative Room. If a student starts to tell a real story, say: “Thank you. We are using the story on the slide. You can talk with me after.” The words used this week (warning, seat change, written reflection, Tap In, Tap Out) are steps, not labels for a person: correct the action, not the child. Welcome responses by writing, drawing, speaking, pointing, signing, or a student’s usual communication tool, and in any language.

Video clips: each “Watch 60 seconds” button opens the YouTube video with captions on and stops at one minute. If a video will not load, read the key message aloud a second time and continue.

Participation moves for every lesson

Equity sticks (cold call): keep one stick per student in a “not yet called” cup. Always give think time first, then say, “Everyone has an answer. I will pull a stick.” Pull, read the name, ask the question, and move the stick to the “called” cup. If a student needs a moment, say “I will come back to you,” call one more student, and return. Accept pointing, signing, or a device response. The scripts name exactly when to pull a stick.

Turn and talk: partners A and B. “Partner A answers first. Partner B, listen so you can repeat it.” After 30 seconds: “Switch.” Then cold call: “Tell us what your partner said.” K–2 partners sit knee to knee for 10 to 15 seconds.

Talk moves: “Say more.” “Who can add on?” “Do you agree or disagree? Why?” “Say it in your own words.” “What in the video or slide makes you think that?”

LiveSchool points: award during the work, name the Safe, Respectful, or Responsible behavior out loud, then tap. Each lesson script includes the exact words. This week, connect points to resets students can see: fixing an action after a reminder, moving to a new seat calmly, writing an honest reflection, and getting back to work after a break. About four positives for every correction. Never award points for being quiet or for finishing first, and never take points for Level 1 behavior during these lessons.

Behavior supports: keep the lesson moving and use the process you are teaching. Give one short warning that names the step, then follow the ladder as written; between steps, tally it and keep moving. Each day’s Transition note has the exact words for the most likely disruption. Safety comes first: any unsafe behavior gets immediate adult support, and the reflection never delays it.

Monday November 9 · Grades K–2 · Indiana SEL 2A Self-Management · 5A Connection

What Gets in the Way of Learning

Target: I can tell when my actions help learning or stop learning.

Key message: Sometimes our bodies want to wiggle, shout, or stop working. That can stop learning for us or for a friend. We can wait, breathe, and get back to learning.

Prepare: Help or Stop? page from the K–2 toolkit (one per student); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: Me Want It (But Me Wait) (Sesame Street).

Evidence: The child sorts helping and stopping pictures and shows one way to wait.

Differentiate: Kindergarten: point to each picture and color with adult support. Grade 1: say “help” or “stop” for each picture. Grade 2: write one sentence about waiting.

Answer notes: Raising a hand and breathing to wait help learning. Shouting out stops learning for friends. Any drawing of waiting, a hand up, or a calm breath is correct.

Take home: Waiting at Home. Family chat: What helps you wait for your turn at home?

What Gets in the Way of Learning

Target: I can name an action and its impact on learning, for me and for others.

Key message: Every behavior has a root: boredom, frustration, not understanding, something on your mind. Different behaviors can have one impact: they put a barrier in front of learning, for you or for someone else.

Prepare: Action and Impact page from the 3–5 toolkit (one per student); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: Don’t eat the marshmallow! (TED).

Evidence: The student names observable actions without labels and states who loses learning time for each.

Differentiate: Grade 3: complete two actions using the chips. Grades 4–5: complete all four and explain the possible root of one. Students may draw and dictate.

Answer notes: Calling out: the class and the teacher lose the lesson. Out of seat: classmates are distracted. Head down: the student misses the learning. Not starting: the student falls behind. A strong return move is specific: hand up, start number one, put a dot and go on.

Take home: Root and Impact. Family chat: What helps you get back on track when something gets in the way?

What Gets in the Way of Learning

Target: I can separate the root of a behavior from its impact, and own the impact in the moment.

Key message: Every behavior has a root, and teen brains are still building impulse control. The root explains. In the moment, the response is to the impact: what the action did to learning, yours or someone else’s.

Prepare: Root and Impact page from the 6–8 toolkit (one per student); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: The mysterious workings of the adolescent brain (TED).

Evidence: The student separates a plausible root from the action and writes a concrete return move that protects learning.

Differentiate: Grade 6: use the root chips for every match. Grades 7–8: explain why understanding the root does not cancel the impact. Accept oral responses.

Answer notes: Roots vary and stay private; any reasonable match is accepted. Shared impact: a barrier to learning, for the student or for someone else in the room. Return moves: dot the hard problem, ask for a quick check, one minute to reset at the seat, phone away.

Take home: Root and Impact. Family chat: When you were my age, what got in the way of your learning, and what helped?

Tap In, Tap Out, and Coming Back

Target: I can explain Level 2 and Level 3 and own a strong return: reconnect, action, impact, correction, return.

Key message: Tap In brings support into the room early so the lesson keeps going. Tap Out is a short step out with support. Level 3 is administrative and goes to the Restorative Room; safety always comes first. Every path ends the same way: a two-minute return and a clean slate. You fell short, but then you bounce back. So you choose.

Prepare: The Two-Minute Return page from the 6–8 toolkit (one per student); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: You Can Learn Anything (Khan Academy).

Evidence: The student orders the five return steps, distinguishes Level 2 from Level 3, and writes return lines that own the action and restart work.

Differentiate: Grade 6: order the steps using the chips. Grades 7–8: explain why the return conversation never adds a new consequence. Accept oral responses.

Answer notes: Order: reconnect, action, impact, correction, return. Level 2: Tap In (support comes in), Tap Out (short step out with support). Level 3: administrative, the Restorative Room. Strong lines: “I was off task.” “It stopped the lesson.” “Next time I’ll use my signal.” “Where do I start?”
