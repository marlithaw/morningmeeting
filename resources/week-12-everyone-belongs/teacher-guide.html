Teach Disability Awareness Week

Monday through Thursday, each grade band follows the day’s theme, key message, YouTube clip, think-and-talk prompt, worksheet, and take-home card. Friday’s lesson, Kindness into Action, runs as Friday’s Morning Spark.

Timing: Welcome ready screen; Regulate 2, Orient 2, Connect 3 (Look 2 and Share 1), Practice 3, Commit 3, Transition 2 minutes. The daily routine (watch and learn, discuss, worksheet, share) runs inside Connect, Practice, and Commit.

Respect and privacy, every day: use the fictional examples on the slides. Never ask a student to name, explain, or show their own or a family member’s disability, diagnosis, or health needs, and never invite students to identify a classmate. Use person-first language: “people with disabilities,” “a student who uses a communication device.” Welcome responses by writing, drawing, speaking, pointing, signing, or a student’s usual communication tool.

Video clips: each “Watch 60 seconds” button opens the YouTube video with captions on and stops at one minute. If a video will not load, read the key message aloud a second time and continue.

Participation moves for every lesson

Equity sticks (cold call): keep one stick per student in a “not yet called” cup. Always give think time first, then say, “Everyone has an answer. I will pull a stick.” Pull, read the name, ask the question, and move the stick to the “called” cup. If a student needs a moment, say “I will come back to you,” call one more student, and return. Accept pointing, signing, or a device response. On Monday through Thursday the scripts name exactly when to pull a stick.

Turn and talk: standing partners A and B. “Partner A answers first. Partner B, listen so you can repeat it.” After 30 seconds: “Switch.” Then cold call: “Tell us what your partner said.” K–2 partners sit knee to knee for 10 to 15 seconds.

Talk moves: “Say more.” “Who can add on?” “Do you agree or disagree? Why?” “Say it in your own words.” “What in the video or slide makes you think that?”

LiveSchool points: award during the work, name the Safe, Respectful, or Responsible behavior out loud, then tap. Each lesson script includes the exact words. Never award points for being quiet or for finishing first, and never take points for Level 1 behavior during these lessons.

Behavior supports: keep the lesson moving and correct privately. Use proximity, a quiet name, and a direct reminder of the expectation. If a student laughs, repeats a hurtful word, or points at a peer, respond in one calm sentence (each day’s Transition note has the exact words), then follow up after the meeting through the restorative process. Any report of teasing or bullying is handled under campus reporting procedures the same day.

Monday October 26 · Grades K–2 · Indiana SEL 5A and 5B · Connection

Everyone Belongs

Target: I can welcome someone and offer a place to join.

Key message: A disability may affect how someone learns, talks, hears, sees, or moves. We cannot always see it. Everyone belongs.

Prepare: Welcome Ticket worksheet (Disability Awareness packet, page 2); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: Lots of Ways to Be Kind (Sesame Workshop).

Evidence: The child points to or names a welcoming choice and shows one way to invite a classmate.

Differentiate: Kindergarten: point to the two picture choices on the slide, then circle on the Welcome Ticket with adult support. Grade 1: say the welcoming words aloud. Grade 2: write the welcoming words on the ticket. Drawing, pointing, and dictating all count.

Answer notes: Wave hello and offer a place to join are welcoming choices. “Go away” is not.

Take home: My Welcome Ticket. Family chat: What could we say to help someone feel welcome?

Everyone Belongs

Target: I can change how a group works so everyone has a real way to participate.

Key message: Disability is not always visible. Belonging means respect, choice, and real opportunities to participate.

Prepare: Belonging in Real Life worksheet (Disability Awareness packet, page 12); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: What is the Hidden Disabilities Sunflower? (Hidden Disabilities Sunflower).

Differentiate: Grade 6: use the frame “Would you like to… or…?”. Grades 7–8: explain how each change affects who gets heard in the group. Writing stays private unless the student chooses to share.

Answer notes: Useful changes: allow more response time and offer different ways to contribute. “Assign no meaningful role” is not useful. A respectful invitation offers, then accepts the answer.

Take home: Belonging Beyond School. Family chat: Why can’t appearance tell us all of someone’s needs?

Different Ways to Learn and Communicate

Target: I can use accurate information to correct a myth about learning differences.

Key message: Dyslexia is one example of a disability that may not be visible. A reading difficulty does not define a person’s intelligence.

Prepare: Myth Busters worksheet (Disability Awareness packet, page 13); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: What Is Dyslexia? (TED-Ed).

Evidence: The student marks myth, fact, myth and explains one answer with information from the video or slide, not a guess about a classmate.

Differentiate: Grade 6: use the frame “This is a myth because…”. Grades 7–8: connect the explanation to why two learners might use different tools. Students may respond orally or with audio support.

Answer notes: On the worksheet: reading difficulty tells us intelligence: MYTH. Audio and other tools can support access: FACT. Everyone with the same disability needs identical help: MYTH.

Take home: Explain It Simply. Family chat: Why might two learners use different tools?

Take home: Access at Home. Family chat: How can one small change make an activity more accessible?

Welcome · ready screen

Say: “Good morning. Fair means everyone has a way to take part. This week is Disability Awareness Week at Matchbook. Today’s focus is Fairness and Access.” Use the usual greeting. The recurring First 5 video may play before the timed meeting. Ground rule for the whole week: use the examples on the slides. No one is asked to talk about their own or a family member’s health or disability, and no one points to a classmate.

Regulate · 2:00

Say: “Let’s get our bodies ready to learn together. Breathe in slowly. Breathe out slowly.” Model three comfortable breaths. Students may breathe, look at the slide, or rest their hands; no one has to close their eyes or make eye contact. Then read the third card aloud: “You belong here.” Keep individual supports such as fidgets, seats, and visual schedules available as usual.

Orient · 2:00

Say: “Today’s goal: I can identify a barrier and propose an accommodation that removes it.” Point to each picture and read its label. Ask students to read the goal with you. Check for understanding with an equity stick: “Which picture shows something you could do today?” Accept pointing. Say: “Yes. We will practice that right now.”

Connect · Look · 2:00

Read the message on the slide aloud, then press the read-aloud button for Spanish if your class needs it. Before the clip, say: “Watch for this question: What helps someone participate?” Press “Watch 60 seconds.” The clip opens with captions on and stops at 1:00 (Awkward Bob: Reasonable Accommodations, DC Office of Disability Rights). If the video does not load, read the message on the slide a second time and go straight to the question. After the clip, give five seconds of think time, then pull an equity stick: “What helps someone participate?” Talk move: “Can you say more?” Then: “Who can add on to that?”

Connect · Share · 1:00

Read the situation aloud: “A group video has spoken instructions and no captions.” Then read each question: What barrier could this create? What change could make the information accessible? Who could benefit from the change? Give ten seconds of silent think time. Turn and talk: “Partner A answers the first question. Partner B answers the second. Go.” After 30 seconds, say “Switch.” Equity stick: pull two sticks. Ask the first student, “What did your partner say?” Ask the second, “Do you agree or want to add on? Say more.” Close with the slide line: “Access means removing barriers.” Strong response: Missing captions are the barrier. Captions or a text transcript are possible changes. They help people who are deaf or hard of hearing, and also anyone in a noisy room, learning English, or who wants to reread the steps.

Practice · Do It · 3:00

Students use the Remove a Barrier worksheet (Disability Awareness packet, page 15). Read each step on the slide aloud and point to where it is on the page. Model the first step in one sentence, then release. 0:00–0:30 model. 0:30–2:30 students work; circulate and read the page aloud for anyone who wants it. 2:30–3:00 two students share. LiveSchool · Responsible: “You found a barrier and designed a fix. That is responsible problem solving.” Award points for the behavior while circulating, not for finishing first. Look for: The worksheet names the missing captions as the barrier, a workable change, and at least one group of people who benefit. Access: Grade 6: use the frame on the slide. Grades 7–8: name a second group who benefits and explain why accommodations are fair, not extra. Accept oral responses.

Commit · 3:00

Say: “The bottom of your page is a take-home card: Access at Home. Tonight, ask your family the family chat question.” Read the family chat question and the frame aloud. Model: “Barrier: the steps are only spoken. Change: turn on captions and post the steps. Benefits: anyone who is deaf or hard of hearing, and anyone who missed a step.” Give 90 seconds to finish the frame by drawing, writing, or telling a partner. Commitments stay private unless a student chooses to share. Pull one equity stick for a volunteer share; a student may pass by saying “I am still thinking.” Students fold the page and put it in their take-home folder.

Transition · 2:00

Say: “Today, notice one barrier and one way to remove it.” Name the next class task and where to start. Use your established release cue. Behavior support: If a student says a support is “unfair” or “cheating,” use it as a teaching moment: “Fair means everyone gets what they need to take part. Glasses are not cheating for someone who needs them.” Keep the focus on supports in general, not on any classmate’s accommodations.
