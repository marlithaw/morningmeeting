Monday · Celebrating with Others

September 14, 2026 · 15 minutes

How can we help someone feel valued when a celebration is new to us?

Grades K–2 · Complete script and activity

Read every prompt aloud. Accept a drawing, gesture, dictated sentence, or quiet observation. Use one short question at a time. Students may choose a classroom example instead of a personal story.

1. Launch · 0:30

Today our question is: How can we help someone feel valued when a celebration is new to us? You can think, talk, draw, or listen. We will learn something and practice one way to use it.

Facilitate: Read the title and essential question. Tell students that listening and passing are welcome forms of participation.

Look for: Students can state today’s focus in their own words.

2. Regulate · 1:00

Let us get ready to hear each other. Feel where your feet rest. Take a comfortable breath if you would like. You may simply sit quietly. Let us leave a little space before we speak.

Facilitate: Give 15 seconds to settle, model two easy breaths, then allow a quiet pause. Do not require closed eyes or a particular posture.

Look for: Students have time to settle and attention returns without forcing the same body position.

3. Orient · 2:00

A celebration can be special to someone even when it is new to us. We can listen and ask, “What do you like about it?” We can watch, join when invited, or say, “No, thank you.” Tomorrow begins Hispanic Heritage Month. We will learn about people and stories together.

Facilitate: Read the student-facing cards and use the script. Pause for one check: “What might a kind guest ask?” Background for teacher reference: Tomorrow, September 15, begins Hispanic Heritage Month. Today prepares us to learn with others. Celebrating with someone can mean listening, showing interest, or appreciating what matters to them. Participation is a choice. Use an invented celebration or a neutral personal example. Never assign a student the role of explaining a culture.

Look for: Students identify the main concept and can name a supporting detail.

4. Watch & notice · 3:00

Use the grade-band video and question in the media table above. Introduce the question, play the recommended segment (up to 2 minutes), then invite one observation. If blocked, discuss the still image and reread the factual cards. See slide 4 Notes for full guidance.

5. Connect · 1:00

Take a quiet moment with this question. You may use a classroom example, a book, or an imagined situation. Partner A has a turn, then Partner B. A pass is okay.

Facilitate: Give 10 seconds to think, 20 seconds per partner, and time for one voluntary response. Do not invite the class to guess someone’s culture or family practices.

Look for: Partners take turns without ranking experiences or pressing for private information.

6. Practice · 4:00

Here is our practice situation. A classmate shares a family song. You have never heard it before. Practice a kind question. Then switch turns. We will explain our choices, try them, and make them clearer.

Facilitate: Minute 1: read and model. Minutes 2–3: Partners take turns being a guest and a person sharing an imaginary favorite song. The guest asks one kind question and listens. Then switch. Minute 4: hear one example and use this answer guidance to clarify and rehearse once more: A kind question asks what the song means to that person. A repair can be: “I am sorry I called it weird. It is new to me.”

Look for: A kind question asks what the song means to that person. A repair can be: “I am sorry I called it weird. It is new to me.”

Today begins Hispanic Heritage Month. It lasts from September 15 to October 15. We learn about Hispanic and Latino people, their stories, and things they have contributed. Heritage includes stories and traditions people carry and share. These communities have roots in many places. Families can enjoy different things. We can learn about someone by listening instead of guessing.

Facilitate: Read the student-facing cards and use the script. Pause for one check: “Does one family tell us about everyone?” Background for teacher reference: Hispanic Heritage Month runs September 15 through October 15. September 15 marks independence anniversaries for Costa Rica, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, and Nicaragua. Mexico observes its independence anniversary September 16 and Chile September 18. The month honors histories, cultures, and contributions in the United States. Heritage is broader than food or festivals. Hispanic and Latino communities include many races, languages, national backgrounds, and individual identities. People do not have to speak Spanish to have Hispanic heritage. Avoid inferring anyone’s identity from their name, appearance, or language. For older students, explain that Hispanic often refers to heritage connected to Spanish-speaking cultures, while Latino refers to Latin American roots. These terms overlap, and people choose their own terms. Puerto Rico is a U.S. territory, not an independent country.
