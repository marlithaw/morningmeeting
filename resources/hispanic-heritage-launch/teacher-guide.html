Celebrating with Others

Grades K–2

Student slidesPrint toolkit

Regulation set-up: Choose a body-weather word or point: sunny, cloudy, stormy. Feel the chair or floor supporting you.

Warm, clear coaching: If a student calls a tradition “weird,” say: “It is new to you. Try a question about what it means.” Invite a repair without demanding personal disclosure.
Regulate · 2:00

Settle together

Say: Choose a body-weather word or point: sunny, cloudy, stormy. Feel the chair or floor supporting you.

Do: Use the full two minutes to welcome and settle: Choose a body-weather word or point: sunny, cloudy, stormy. Feel the chair or floor supporting you. Eyes may stay open. Breathing and sharing are optional.

Orient · 2:00

Name the purpose

Say: A celebration can be special to someone even when it is new to us. We can listen and ask, “What do you like about it?” We can watch, join when invited, or say, “No, thank you.” Tomorrow begins Hispanic Heritage Month. We will learn about people and stories together.

Do: Read the student-facing cards and use the script. Pause for one check: “What might a kind guest ask?” Background for teacher reference: Tomorrow, September 15, begins Hispanic Heritage Month. Today prepares us to learn with others. Celebrating with someone can mean listening, showing interest, or appreciating what matters to them. Participation is a choice. Use an invented celebration or a neutral personal example. Never assign a student the role of explaining a culture.

Connect · Share · 1:00

Listen & connect

Say: Take a quiet moment with this question. You may use a classroom example, a book, or an imagined situation. Partner A has a turn, then Partner B. A pass is okay.

Do: Allow 10 seconds to think, 20 seconds per partner, and 10 seconds to name one connection. Passing is welcome.

Practice · 3:00

Rehearse the skill

Say: Here is our practice situation. A classmate shares a family song. You have never heard it before. Practice a kind question. Then switch turns. We will explain our choices, try them, and make them clearer.

Do: Minute 1: model the prompt. Minute 2: partners rehearse or respond. Minute 3: hear one example and clarify. A kind question asks what the song means to that person. A repair can be: “I am sorry I called it weird. It is new to me.”

Commit · 3:00

Choose an action

Say: What will you actually do today? Choose something specific. “Be nice” can become “Let someone finish before I ask a question.” You can keep your commitment private.

Do: Minute 1: choose an action. Minute 2: say, draw, or write the commitment. Minute 3: rehearse or refine it. Sharing may remain private.

Transition · 2:00

Carry it forward

Grades K–2

Student slidesPrint toolkit

Do: Use the full two minutes to welcome and settle: Trace a finger slowly up and down while taking an easy breath, or watch quietly. Eyes may stay open. Breathing and sharing are optional.

Orient · 2:00

Name the purpose

Say: Today begins Hispanic Heritage Month. It lasts from September 15 to October 15. We learn about Hispanic and Latino people, their stories, and things they have contributed. Heritage includes stories and traditions people carry and share. These communities have roots in many places. Families can enjoy different things. We can learn about someone by listening instead of guessing.

Do: Read the student-facing cards and use the script. Pause for one check: “Does one family tell us about everyone?” Background for teacher reference: Hispanic Heritage Month runs September 15 through October 15. September 15 marks independence anniversaries for Costa Rica, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, and Nicaragua. Mexico observes its independence anniversary September 16 and Chile September 18. The month honors histories, cultures, and contributions in the United States. Heritage is broader than food or festivals. Hispanic and Latino communities include many races, languages, national backgrounds, and individual identities. People do not have to speak Spanish to have Hispanic heritage. Avoid inferring anyone’s identity from their name, appearance, or language. For older students, explain that Hispanic often refers to heritage connected to Spanish-speaking cultures, while Latino refers to Latin American roots. These terms overlap, and people choose their own terms. Puerto Rico is a U.S. territory, not an independent country.

Name the purpose

They contributed in different ways. A contribution is something a person adds or does that helps. Let us remember what each person did.

Do: Read the student-facing cards and use the script. Ellen Ochoa became the first Hispanic woman in space in 1993 as a NASA astronaut. Roberto Clemente was born in Puerto Rico and was both a baseball player and a humanitarian who used his success to support others. These are two examples among many contributions, not representatives of every Hispanic or Latino experience. Treat the discussion of what students find inspiring as their interpretation.

Stories Help Us Belong

Grades K–2

Student slidesPrint toolkit

Regulate · 2:00

Settle together

Say: Notice your feet or hands resting. Choose one comfortable breath before naming your intention.

Do: Use the full two minutes to welcome and settle: Notice your feet or hands resting. Choose one comfortable breath before naming your intention. Eyes may stay open. Breathing and sharing are optional.

Orient · 2:00

Name the purpose

Say: Pura Belpré was a librarian from Puerto Rico. She became the first Puerto Rican librarian at the New York Public Library. She shared Puerto Rican stories with children. Today we will practice listening to a story and remembering a detail. We can share a favorite book, invent a character, or choose to listen.

Do: Read the student-facing cards and use the script. Pause for one check: “What did Belpré share through her library work?” Background for teacher reference: Pura Belpré began work at the New York Public Library in 1921 as its first Puerto Rican librarian. She shared Puerto Rican folktales with children and published stories. Connect that documented work to today’s question about listening and access to stories. Students may discuss a book, invent a story, or pass. Personal heritage sharing and family interviews are not required. The class can learn from published sources without placing the burden on classmates.

Connect · Share · 1:00

Listen & connect

Say: Take a quiet moment with this question. You may use a classroom example, a book, or an imagined situation. Partner A has a turn, then Partner B. A pass is okay.

Do: Allow 10 seconds to think, 20 seconds per partner, and 10 seconds to name one connection. Passing is welcome.

Practice · 3:00

Rehearse the skill

Say: Here is our practice situation. A partner tells you about a story. Your job is to listen carefully. Take turns. Sharing is a choice. We will explain our choices, try them, and make them clearer.

Do: Minute 1: model the prompt. Minute 2: partners rehearse or respond. Minute 3: hear one example and clarify. Belpré worked as a librarian and shared Puerto Rican stories. Listening looks like waiting, recalling a detail, and thanking the speaker.
