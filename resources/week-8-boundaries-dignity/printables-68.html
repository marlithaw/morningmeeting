THE BOUNDARY ANALYSIS CARD

Permission What observable evidence shows agreement?

Dignity Is someone's body, clothing, privacy, or embarrassment becoming entertainment?

Safety Could this create physical risk or activate a threat response?

Crowd Are observers increasing or reducing the harm?

ACCOUNTABILITY TEST

Before calling it repair, ask:

Did I name my action accurately? Did I remove excuses? Did I complete the repair expected of me? Can my future behavior show change?

Direct only when safe. Get adult help for ongoing harm. Check in afterward without demanding details.

Monday · September 21 · Grades 6–8 · Student tool

My Body. My Boundary.

I can: Distinguish observable permission from assumptions based on friendship, history, silence, or laughter.

Scenario: “We're close friends. I always mess with his hoodie. He knows I'm playing.”

SEE IT: What happened?

NAME IT: What decision was made for the other person?

DO IT: Rewrite the interaction so autonomy stays intact.

Words and decisions only. No touching or reenactment. You may pass.

Observable evidence:

Assumption I need to remove:

Rewrite with a choice and an accepted answer:

My commitment

One observable way I will respect bodily autonomy today is ____.

Monday · Grades 6–8 · Teacher companion

Model · Prompt · Check

Have ready

Provide a two-column EVIDENCE / ASSUMPTION organizer and the hoodie statement. Students may write privately.

Say and model

“‘We are close friends’ is relationship information, not evidence of permission now. ‘He knows I am playing’ assumes another person’s decision.”

Three-minute practice

0:00–1:00: identify the first assumption. 1:00–2:00: annotate the second and rewrite the interaction with a question, a pause, and an accepted answer. 2:00–3:00: explain who controls the choice in the rewrite.

Worked answer

The hoodie statement gives no clear permission for the current contact. A respectful rewrite asks first and leaves the hoodie alone if the answer is no, unclear, or absent. In the visual cases, an explicit yes and an accepted no are evidence; reaching without asking is not.

Response scaffold

“The statement ____ assumes ____. Clear permission would require ____. If the answer is no or unclear, ____.” Allow circling/labeling before writing.

Look for

The analysis separates what is observable from what is assumed and gives the other person an unpressured choice.

If the response misses the skill

If a student relies on “we always do this,” ask: “What evidence answers the question today?” Silence or nervous laughter cannot supply the answer.

Extend when ready

Explain why permission can change without the person having to justify the change.

Expected exit response

Permission is freely and clearly given; friendship or a past answer does not substitute for it.

When A Prank Crosses The Line

I can: Separate intent from impact and sequence an immediate response to a boundary violation.

Jordan pulls Marcus's sweatpants down in front of a crowd. Students laugh and some raise phones. Marcus gets dressed and leaves without speaking.

SEE IT: Facts only.

NAME IT: Permission · Dignity · Safety · Crowd impact.

DO IT: Identify the first three actions that should happen next.

Words and decisions only. No touching or reenactment. You may pass.

Facts (no inferred feelings):

Permission / Dignity / Safety / Crowd evidence:

First action → Second action → Third action:

My commitment

If an action removes someone's choice, privacy, or safety, I will ____.

Tuesday · Grades 6–8 · Teacher companion

Model · Prompt · Check

Have ready

Provide FACT / INTERPRETATION and Permission / Dignity / Safety / Crowd columns. Read the supplied hallway scenario as text only.

Say and model

“Clothing was pulled down and phones were raised” is observable. “Marcus did not care” is an inference; leaving silently does not establish it.

Three-minute practice

0:00–1:00: model a fact versus an inference. 1:00–2:00: partners mark one detail per analysis category. 2:00–3:00: order three immediate actions and explain why the sequence protects privacy and safety.

Worked answer

1. Stop the violation safely and protect privacy. 2. Disperse the audience, put phones away, and bring adult support; do not seize phones or confront unsafely. 3. Check privately with the affected student. Silence does not prove agreement or absence of harm.

Response scaffold

“The observable detail is ____. It affects ____ because ____. First ____, then ____, then ____.” Students may annotate rather than discuss publicly.

Look for

Students do not require visible distress as proof, distinguish impact from intent, and include audience/recording behavior in the response.

If the response misses the skill

If someone says “Nobody got hurt,” ask: “What happened to clothing, privacy, choice, and the audience? Physical injury is not the only concern.”

Extend when ready

Identify how the first response changes if Direct would create a safety risk: prioritize adult help and privacy without confrontation.

Expected exit response

Intent explains a purpose; it does not erase lost choice, exposure, or risk.

The Crowd Has Power

I can: Choose a safe first intervention and a distinct afterward support action, using the 4 Ds.

A hallway crowd forms while a student attempts to pull down a younger student's clothing. Phones begin to come out.

Choose one first move: Direct / Distract / Delegate.

Then identify a Delay move for afterward.

Explain why your sequence is safe and useful.

Words and decisions only. No touching or reenactment. You may pass.

NOW: D + exact words + safety reason:

AFTERWARD: Delay action + offer of choice:

How my sequence reduces the audience or brings support:

My commitment

If I see a boundary violation, my first safe move will be ____.

Wednesday · Grades 6–8 · Teacher companion

Model · Prompt · Check

Have ready

Provide a NOW / AFTERWARD organizer with Direct, Distract, Delegate, and Delay choices. Include the younger student, hallway audience, and phones in the written case.

Say and model

“I choose Delegate now because an adult needs to stop the ongoing violation. I can also avoid adding an audience. Later I can offer to walk with the student to support.”

Three-minute practice

0:00–1:00: compare safety of Direct and Delegate. 1:00–2:00: partners choose a first move and an afterward move, with one reason each. 2:00–3:00: rehearse the exact brief words and revise any threat, insult, or performance.

Worked answer

Delegate: “I need an adult at the hallway now; someone is pulling a student’s clothing.” Distract can reduce the crowd if safe. Direct is not required. Delay: “Do you want me to walk with you to an adult?” Do not wait until later to seek help for ongoing harm.

Response scaffold

“First I will ____ because ____. My words are ____. Afterward I will ____ without demanding details.” Private written rehearsal is acceptable.

Look for

The sequence reduces harm, does not increase physical risk, addresses the audience, and offers rather than imposes afterward support.

If the response misses the skill

If a student proposes recording, say: “We protect privacy. What useful action brings support without turning this into content?”

Extend when ready

Compare two safe sequences and explain which fits the setting and available adult support.

Expected exit response

Useful intervention reduces harm, protects dignity, and brings support; it does not require a public confrontation.

Repair Is An Action

I can: Construct an accountability statement with behavior, impact, appropriate repair, and evidence of future change.

Instead of: “It was just a prank. Nobody got hurt.”

Try: “I crossed a body boundary and made another person's private space public. That was my choice. I will follow the repair plan and change how I act around their body and space.”

Test it: Does it name the behavior? Remove excuses? Address repair? Identify change?

Repair action + future evidence:

My commitment

One observable action I will take to protect bodily autonomy and dignity is ____.

Thursday · Grades 6–8 · Teacher companion

Model · Prompt · Check

Have ready

Provide a four-part audit: NAME ACTION / REMOVE EXCUSES / REPAIR / FUTURE EVIDENCE. Use a fictional response only.

Say and model

Cross out “just a prank” as a minimizer. Replace “nobody got hurt” with the observable boundary and privacy impact. Do not invent the affected student’s feelings.

Three-minute practice

0:00–1:00: model removing one excuse. 1:00–2:00: students rewrite the statement and mark each of the four required elements. 2:00–3:00: partners or the teacher identify one missing element and revise it.

Worked answer

“I pulled clothing without permission and exposed someone’s private space. That was my choice. I will complete the adult-supported repair plan and respect the person’s requested distance. I will keep my hands off others’ clothing and ask before contact.” Future evidence is repeated changed behavior, not a demand for forgiveness.

Response scaffold

“I did ____. It affected ____. I will repair by ____. Next time I will ____.” Optional private writing; label the four parts without reading the statement aloud.

Look for

The response names a specific action, acknowledges impact without speculation, removes blame shifting, and identifies repair and observable change.

If the response misses the skill

If the statement uses “but,” “everybody,” “they made me,” or “it was not serious,” ask: “Which part names your choice without reducing responsibility?”

Extend when ready

Explain why repair may occur without a face-to-face conversation or the harmed person’s participation.

Expected exit response

Sorry can begin repair. Accurate ownership, an appropriate repair action, and a changed pattern demonstrate it.
