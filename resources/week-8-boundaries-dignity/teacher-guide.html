Teacher guide & scripts

My body belongs to me. Your body belongs to you. Our community protects both.

MondayTuesdayWednesdayThursday

Before teaching

Regulate 2 · Orient 2 · Connect 3 · Practice 3 · Commit 3 · Transition 2 = 15 minutes. Welcome is an untimed ready screen. Review notes before projecting: N opens notes on the student screen. Keep this guide on a separate teacher device when needed.

1. Do not ask, “Who has had this happen?” “Who has done this?” or anything else that solicits public disclosure.

2. Do not use names or identifying details from real school incidents.

3. Students never physically reenact pantsing, grabbing, clothing-pulling, blocking, restraint, or any other boundary violation.

4. Practice focuses on language, decision-making, moving away, getting help, and checking in.

5. Nervous laughter does not automatically mean someone thinks an incident is funny. Do not ask students to infer consent from facial expression.

6. A student who experienced harm is not responsible for comforting the person who caused it or participating in a face-to-face repair.

7. If a disclosure occurs, acknowledge it briefly and calmly, protect the student's privacy, and follow established school reporting/support procedures outside the public lesson.

8. Avoid blanket legal labels. The universal instructional point is precise enough: deliberately pulling clothing, exposing someone's body, or unwanted touching is a serious bodily-boundary and dignity violation requiring adult response.

Monday · September 21

My Body. My Boundary.

K–2: Students will identify that each person controls their own body and demonstrate Ask → Wait → Respect.

3–5: Students will explain bodily autonomy in concrete language and determine when permission is needed.

6–8: Students will define bodily autonomy and consent and distinguish clear permission from assumption.

Grades K–2

Student slidesPrint toolkit

Slide 1 · Welcome · 0:00

Script: “Today we're talking about something every person in this room has: a body and the right to make choices about it. Our question is: What does it look like to respect another person's body and space? You will not be asked to tell personal stories. We are going to learn a skill and practice it.”

Look for: Students understand that the lesson concerns everyday respect, not punishment or investigation.

Access: Read aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictation, a partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a personal story.

Practice boundary: Fictional situations only. No personal disclosures or physical reenactments.

Slide 2 · Regulate · 2:00

Script: “Before we talk about boundaries, notice your own space. You do not need to close your eyes or take a deep breath. Feel what is supporting your body. Notice where your hands, feet, chair, or body are in relation to other people. Make any small adjustment that helps you feel ready while keeping everyone's space protected.”

Facilitation: Do not ask children to stretch arms into “personal bubbles.” The concept can be learned without bringing bodies closer together.

Look for: Students settle while maintaining physical boundaries.

Access: Read aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictation, a partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a personal story.

Practice boundary: Fictional situations only. No personal disclosures or physical reenactments.

Slide 3 · Orient · 2:00

K–2 script: “Your body belongs to you. That means people ask before touching. If someone asks you for a hug, high five, or other touch, you can say yes, no, or not now. When we hear someone's answer, we respect it.”

3–5 script: “Bodily autonomy means each person has the right to make decisions about their own body. Your clothing is also part of your physical boundary. Friendship does not remove the need to respect someone's space.”

6–8 script: “Autonomy is control over your own body. Consent is clear permission. We do not turn silence, embarrassment, friendship, nervous laughter, or what happened last time into permission for this time.”

Check: “If someone said yes yesterday, what do we know about today?” Expected: We still need to respect today's choice.

Access: Read aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictation, a partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a personal story.

Practice boundary: Fictional situations only. No personal disclosures or physical reenactments.

Slide 4 · Connect · Look · 2:00

Script: “Don't decide whether each person is ‘nice’ or ‘mean.’ Look only at the evidence. Who asked? Who answered? Who respected the answer?”

Facilitation: Give 20–30 seconds of silent noticing before soliciting responses.

Look for: Students use observable evidence rather than “they probably didn't care.”

Access: Read aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictation, a partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a personal story.

Practice boundary: Fictional situations only. No personal disclosures or physical reenactments.

Slide 5 · Connect · Share · 1:00

Script: “Think first. You may use an invented example. Partner A gets a turn, then Partner B. A pass is okay.”

Look for: Students connect respect to behaviors such as asking, listening, stopping, giving room, or accepting an answer.

Access: Read aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictation, a partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a personal story.

Practice boundary: Fictional situations only. No personal disclosures or physical reenactments.

Slide 6 · Practice · 3:00

Script: “Our skill is not complicated: See it. Name it. Do it. First notice the situation. Then name whose boundary or choice is involved. Then choose the respectful action.”

K–2 facilitation: Teacher models both “yes” and “no.” Students practice responding “Okay” without pouting, bargaining, or demanding a reason.

3–5 facilitation: Emphasize that touching a pencil and putting an arm around a person are not the same type of decision.

6–8 facilitation: Challenge “We're friends” and “we always do this” without shaming. Ask: “Who is making the decision in that sentence?”

Look for: The student's alternative returns the choice to the other person.

Access: Read aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictation, a partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a personal story.

Practice boundary: Fictional situations only. No personal disclosures or physical reenactments.

Slide 7 · Commit · 3:00

Script: “Choose something specific enough that another person could see it. ‘Be respectful’ is a goal. ‘Ask before touching someone's things or body’ is an action.”

Look for: Observable commitments.

Access: Read aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictation, a partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a personal story.

Practice boundary: Fictional situations only. No personal disclosures or physical reenactments.

Slide 8 · Transition · 2:00

Script: “Before we move on, say the idea in your own words. What comes before touching another person or their clothing? Carry your commitment into the next part of the day.”

Mastery evidence: Students can explain that another person's body requires that person's choice.

Access: Read aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictation, a partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a personal story.

Practice boundary: Fictional situations only. No personal disclosures or physical reenactments.

Tuesday · September 22

When A Prank Crosses The Line

K–2: Students will recognize that play must be safe and wanted by everyone involved.

3–5: Students will use a three-question diagnostic—Permission, Dignity, Safety—to determine when joking crosses a boundary.

6–8: Students will distinguish intent from impact and analyze how humiliation, exposure, and crowd behavior change the nature of an incident.

The source curriculum explicitly contrasts ordinary celebration/minor joking with clothing-pulling and public exposure, and emphasizes dignity, physical safety, and the body's threat response.

Grades K–2

Student slidesPrint toolkit

Slide 1 · Welcome · 0:00

Script: “Yesterday we learned who gets to decide what happens to a person's body. Today we apply that idea to joking and pranks. Our question is not ‘Did someone mean to be funny?’ Our question is ‘What actually happened to another person's choice, dignity, and safety?’”

Access: Read aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictation, a partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a personal story.

Practice boundary: Fictional situations only. No personal disclosures or physical reenactments.

Slide 2 · Regulate · 2:00

Script: “When a crowd laughs, our brains sometimes want to join before we have even decided what we think. Give yourself a moment to notice before reacting.”

K–2 language: “You never have to laugh just because somebody else is laughing.”

Look for: Students can pause without teacher prescribing a particular emotional response.

Access: Read aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictation, a partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a personal story.

Practice boundary: Fictional situations only. No personal disclosures or physical reenactments.

Slide 3 · Orient · 2:00

Script: “We need something stronger than ‘I thought it was funny.’ We are going to use three questions.”

For 3–8, point to each word:

Permission: “Did the person clearly agree?” Dignity: “Did the action protect their privacy and humanity?” Safety: “Could this make their body or the environment unsafe?”

6–8 extension: “Bodies can respond to a sudden boundary violation with fight, flight, or freeze. Someone does not have to cry, yell, or fight back for the action to be serious.”

Permission check: A high-five still requires agreement; do not infer it from friendship or a celebration. Stop words matter, and silence or laughter is not permission. If agreement is unclear, ask and wait.

Access: Read aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictation, a partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a personal story.

Practice boundary: Fictional situations only. No personal disclosures or physical reenactments.

Slide 4 · Connect · Look · 2:00

Script: “Classify the behavior, not the person. Do not call anyone a bully, predator, bad kid, or victim. Tell me which evidence changes your conclusion.”

3–5 misconception check: “If the person laughs after being embarrassed, does that automatically equal permission?” Expected: No.

6–8 misconception check: “If nobody was punched, can dignity or safety still be violated?” Expected: Yes.

Permission check: A high-five still requires agreement; do not infer it from friendship or a celebration. Stop words matter, and silence or laughter is not permission. If agreement is unclear, ask and wait.

Access: Read aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictation, a partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a personal story.

Practice boundary: Fictional situations only. No personal disclosures or physical reenactments.

Slide 5 · Connect · Share · 1:00

Script: “Ten seconds to think. You may analyze the fictional scenario only. Partner A, then Partner B. Passing is fine.”

Look for: Students recognize stop language, withdrawal, exposure, loss of privacy, or lack of permission as evidence.

Permission check: A high-five still requires agreement; do not infer it from friendship or a celebration. Stop words matter, and silence or laughter is not permission. If agreement is unclear, ask and wait.

Access: Read aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictation, a partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a personal story.

Practice boundary: Fictional situations only. No personal disclosures or physical reenactments.

Slide 6 · Practice · 3:00

K–2 teacher response: “If Sam said stop, we don't need to decide whether Jordan meant to be funny. The action stops.”

3–5 facilitation: Have students show 1–3 fingers as the class checks Permission, Dignity, and Safety. Do not turn the scenario into a debate over whether Marcus “overreacted.”

6–8 facilitation: Ask for three immediate actions:

1. Stop the violation and protect privacy.

2. Remove the audience/phones and get adult support.

3. Check on the affected student privately.

Look for: Students move from judgment to action.

Access: Read aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictation, a partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a personal story.

Practice boundary: Fictional situations only. No personal disclosures or physical reenactments.

Slide 7 · Commit · 3:00

Script: “A commitment should change a decision you might actually make today. Make it concrete.”

Strong examples include asking first, stopping when told, refusing to film, not feeding a crowd, or getting help.

Access: Read aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictation, a partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a personal story.

Practice boundary: Fictional situations only. No personal disclosures or physical reenactments.

Slide 8 · Transition · 2:00

Script: “Say the three-question test with me once: Permission. Dignity. Safety. That is the tool. Carry it with you.”

Mastery evidence: Students can explain why an action can cross a boundary even when someone calls it a joke.

Access: Read aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictation, a partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a personal story.

Practice boundary: Fictional situations only. No personal disclosures or physical reenactments.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS TO PRE-CORRECT

Student statementTeacher response
“But we're friends.”“Friendship does not remove someone's right to choose.”
“They let me do it before.”“Past permission is not automatic permission today.”
“They were laughing.”“Laughter can mean many things. Clear permission is clearer than guessing.”
