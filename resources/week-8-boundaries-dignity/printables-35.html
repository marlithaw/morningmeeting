Grades 3–5 · Cut-apart reference cards

Keep the tool. Practice the action.

Permission · Dignity · Safety

1. Permission: Did the person clearly agree?

2. Dignity: Does the action protect their body and privacy?

3. Safety: Does it keep their body and environment safe?

If one answer is NO → Stop and choose another action. If unclear, pause and ask.

THE 4 Ds

DIRECT: Say what needs to stop.

DISTRACT: Break the momentum.

DELEGATE: Get adult help.

DELAY: Check in afterward.

Direct only when safe. Get adult help for ongoing harm. Check in afterward without demanding details.

Monday · September 21 · Grades 3–5 · Student tool

My Body. My Boundary.

I can: Explain bodily autonomy and identify permission involving bodies, clothing, and belongings.

Scenario A: Borrowing a pencil.

Scenario B: Putting your arm around someone for a picture.

Scenario C: Pulling a friend's hood over their head.

SEE IT → NAME IT → DO IT

Decide which involves a body/clothing boundary. Practice the respectful alternative.

Words and decisions only. No touching or reenactment. You may pass.

Boundary: BODY / CLOTHING / BELONGING

My permission question:

If the answer is no:

My commitment

Today I will protect body boundaries by ____.

Choose something another person could notice.

Monday · Grades 3–5 · Teacher companion

Model · Prompt · Check

Have ready

Provide the pencil, arm-around-for-a-picture, and hood-pulling scenario cards plus BODY / CLOTHING / BELONGING labels.

Say and model

“A pencil is a belonging; I ask to borrow it. An arm around someone is body contact; I ask and wait. A hood is clothing someone is wearing; I keep my hands off and ask.”

Three-minute practice

0:00–1:00: model one card. 1:00–2:00: partners label each remaining boundary and write the exact question they would ask. 2:00–3:00: hear a response and rehearse accepting “No, thanks.”

Worked answer

Pencil: ask to borrow a belonging. Photo: “May I put my arm around you?” Hood: do not pull it over someone’s head; give them the choice. Maya and Zoe ask and respect an answer; Eli guesses instead of asking.

Response scaffold

“This involves ____. I would ask ____. If the answer is no, I will ____.” Offer the three labels to point to before writing.

Look for

Students distinguish a belonging from body/clothing contact and return the decision to the other person.

If the response misses the skill

If someone says friendship is enough, ask: “Where is the answer for this time? Show the words that give the person a choice.”

Extend when ready

Does permission yesterday settle the choice today? Explain why not.

Expected exit response

Ask before touching someone’s body or clothing, wait, and respect the answer.

Tuesday · September 22 · Grades 3–5 · Student tool

When A Prank Crosses The Line

I can: Use Permission, Dignity, and Safety to analyze a specific action and identify an immediate response.

Jordan pulls Marcus's sweatpants down in a hallway while students laugh. Marcus pulls them back up and leaves.

1. Permission? 2. Dignity? 3. Safety?

Then: What should happen immediately?

Words and decisions only. No touching or reenactment. You may pass.

Permission: YES / NO / UNCLEAR. Detail:

Dignity concern and evidence:

Safety concern and immediate action:

My commitment

Before I call something “just a joke,” I will ____.

Tuesday · Grades 3–5 · Teacher companion

Model · Prompt · Check

Have ready

Provide a three-column check sheet. Read the hallway scenario aloud without acting it out. Give YES / NO / UNCLEAR response choices.

Say and model

“The scenario does not show Marcus giving permission. Pulling down clothing exposes private space. Crowding can create additional risk. The action must stop and an adult must respond.”

Three-minute practice

0:00–1:00: model the Permission column. 1:00–2:00: partners complete Dignity and Safety with one detail each. 2:00–3:00: name the first helpful action and explain it.

Worked answer

A high-five after a play does not itself prove permission: ask or identify clear agreement. Returning a hidden pencil pouch is necessary but does not make hiding it welcome. Clothing-pulling that exposes someone is a serious boundary violation: stop, give privacy and space, and get adult support.

Response scaffold

“The detail ____ shows a concern about ____.” Students can underline a detail instead of composing a sentence.

Look for

Students use the action as evidence, name privacy/dignity as well as physical safety, and move from classification to response.

If the response misses the skill

If someone says “Marcus overreacted,” redirect: “We are evaluating Jordan’s action. What happened to permission and privacy?”

Extend when ready

Explain why a person laughing after embarrassment does not prove agreement.

Expected exit response

An intention to joke does not replace permission, dignity, or safety.

Wednesday · September 23 · Grades 3–5 · Student tool

The Crowd Has Power

I can: Select one of the 4 Ds and explain why it is safe and useful.

A group crowds around while someone tries to pull another student's shorts down.

DIRECT: “Stop. Hands off.”

DISTRACT: “Bell's about to ring. Let's move.”

DELEGATE: “I need an adult here now.”

DELAY: “Are you okay? Want me to walk with you?”

Choose one. Explain why. Rehearse it.

Words and decisions only. No touching or reenactment. You may pass.

My D:

Why it fits safely:

The exact words I will use:

My commitment

My safest useful bystander move is ____ because ____.

Wednesday · Grades 3–5 · Teacher companion

Model · Prompt · Check

Have ready

Provide four choice cards with one example phrase each. Establish that students never act out pulling, blocking, or crowding.

Say and model

“I choose Delegate because a boundary violation is happening and an adult is needed. I would say: ‘I need an adult here now. Someone is pulling clothing.’”

Three-minute practice

0:00–1:00: model choosing a D. 1:00–2:00: partners choose another safe move, state a reason, and rehearse only its words. 2:00–3:00: compare the choices and add an afterward check-in.

Worked answer

Direct: “Stop. Hands off,” only if safe. Distract: redirect the audience toward class. Delegate: request an adult promptly. Delay: “Want me to walk with you?” afterward. Do not use Delay as a reason to wait while harm continues.

Response scaffold

“I choose ____ because ____. I would say ____.” Offer Direct / Distract / Delegate / Delay cards with printed phrases.

Look for

The student links the chosen D to safety, uses brief words, and involves an adult for ongoing harm.

If the response misses the skill

If a response threatens someone, say: “Keep the purpose—stop harm—and change the words. Try ‘Give them space’ or get an adult.”

Extend when ready

Explain a situation where Delegate is stronger than Direct.

Expected exit response

Name a D, say what it sounds like, and explain why it helps safely.

Thursday · September 24 · Grades 3–5 · Student tool

Repair Is An Action

I can: Distinguish excuses from ownership and write a specific accountability response.

Instead of: “Everybody was laughing.”

Try: “I crossed a body boundary and embarrassed someone. I need to follow the repair plan and respect their space.”

Task: What makes the second response stronger? Rework one excuse into ownership.

Words and decisions only. No touching or reenactment. You may pass.

I did:

It affected:

I will repair by:

Next time I will:

My commitment

Our community is safer when I ____.

Thursday · Grades 3–5 · Teacher companion

Model · Prompt · Check

Have ready

Give an EXCUSE / OWNERSHIP sort and the four-part accountability frame. Work on a fictional case, not an apology to an actual peer.

Say and model

“Everybody was laughing” moves responsibility to the crowd. “I crossed a body boundary” names the speaker’s own choice.

Three-minute practice

0:00–1:00: model the contrast. 1:00–2:00: partners rewrite the excuse using behavior, impact, repair, and next action. 2:00–3:00: underline ownership and circle the observable change.

Worked answer

“I pulled clothing without permission. That exposed someone’s private space. I will follow the adult-supported repair plan and respect their space. Next time I will keep my hands off and ask before contact.”

Response scaffold

“I did ____. It affected ____. I will repair by ____. Next time I will ____.” Offer ASK FIRST / GIVE SPACE / FOLLOW THE PLAN as action starters.

Look for

The response names the action, avoids an excuse, refers to appropriate repair, and includes a visible future change.

If the response misses the skill

If the answer ends at “I said sorry,” ask: “What action will show the change tomorrow?” Do not require forgiveness or contact with the harmed student.

Extend when ready

Match each accountability sentence to Stop, Own, Repair, or Rebuild.

Expected exit response

An apology becomes believable through honest ownership, repair, and changed behavior.
