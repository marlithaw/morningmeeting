Student slides remain intentionally sparse. Presenter notes carry the precision.

Repeated access language in presenter notes

K–2: Read every prompt aloud. Accept pointing, gesture, drawing, dictated response, partner response, quiet thinking, or a pass. Never require a student to share a personal experience.

3–5: Offer sentence frames orally and visually. Provide quiet thinking time. Students may use a fictional example or pass. Ask for one observable detail to support an answer.

6–8: Allow private writing or a pass. Students may analyze fictional situations instead of personal experiences. Ask students to support conclusions with observable evidence rather than assumptions about another person's feelings.

Teacher guardrails

1. Do not ask, “Who has had this happen?” “Who has done this?” or anything else that solicits public disclosure.

2. Do not use names or identifying details from real school incidents.

3. Students never physically reenact pantsing, grabbing, clothing-pulling, blocking, restraint, or any other boundary violation.

4. Practice focuses on language, decision-making, moving away, getting help, and checking in.

5. Nervous laughter does not automatically mean someone thinks an incident is funny. Do not ask students to infer consent from facial expression.

6. A student who experienced harm is not responsible for comforting the person who caused it or participating in a face-to-face repair.

7. If a disclosure occurs, acknowledge it briefly and calmly, protect the student's privacy, and follow established school reporting/support procedures outside the public lesson.

8. Avoid blanket legal labels. The universal instructional point is precise enough: deliberately pulling clothing, exposing someone's body, or unwanted touching is a serious bodily-boundary and dignity violation requiring adult response.

MONDAY PRESENTER NOTES

01 · Welcome · 0:00

Script: “Today we're talking about something every person in this room has: a body and the right to make choices about it. Our question is: What does it look like to respect another person's body and space? You will not be asked to tell personal stories. We are going to learn a skill and practice it.”

Look for: Students understand that the lesson concerns everyday respect, not punishment or investigation.

02 · Regulate · 2:00

Script: “Before we talk about boundaries, notice your own space. You do not need to close your eyes or take a deep breath. Feel what is supporting your body. Notice where your hands, feet, chair, or body are in relation to other people. Make any small adjustment that helps you feel ready while keeping everyone's space protected.”

Facilitation: Do not ask children to stretch arms into “personal bubbles.” The concept can be learned without bringing bodies closer together.

Look for: Students settle while maintaining physical boundaries.

03 · Orient · 2:00

K–2 script: “Your body belongs to you. That means people ask before touching. If someone asks you for a hug, high five, or other touch, you can say yes, no, or not now. When we hear someone's answer, we respect it.”

3–5 script: “Bodily autonomy means each person has the right to make decisions about their own body. Your clothing is also part of your physical boundary. Friendship does not remove the need to respect someone's space.”

6–8 script: “Autonomy is control over your own body. Consent is clear permission. We do not turn silence, embarrassment, friendship, nervous laughter, or what happened last time into permission for this time.”

Check: “If someone said yes yesterday, what do we know about today?” Expected: We still need to respect today's choice.

04 · Connect · Look · 2:00

Script: “Don't decide whether each person is ‘nice’ or ‘mean.’ Look only at the evidence. Who asked? Who answered? Who respected the answer?”

Facilitation: Give 20–30 seconds of silent noticing before soliciting responses.

Look for: Students use observable evidence rather than “they probably didn't care.”

05 · Connect · Share · 1:00

Script: “Think first. You may use an invented example. Partner A gets a turn, then Partner B. A pass is okay.”

Look for: Students connect respect to behaviors such as asking, listening, stopping, giving room, or accepting an answer.

06 · Practice · 3:00

Script: “Our skill is not complicated: See it. Name it. Do it. First notice the situation. Then name whose boundary or choice is involved. Then choose the respectful action.”

K–2 facilitation: Teacher models both “yes” and “no.” Students practice responding “Okay” without pouting, bargaining, or demanding a reason.

3–5 facilitation: Emphasize that touching a pencil and putting an arm around a person are not the same type of decision.

6–8 facilitation: Challenge “We're friends” and “we always do this” without shaming. Ask: “Who is making the decision in that sentence?”

Look for: The student's alternative returns the choice to the other person.

07 · Commit · 3:00

Script: “Choose something specific enough that another person could see it. ‘Be respectful’ is a goal. ‘Ask before touching someone's things or body’ is an action.”

Look for: Observable commitments.

08 · Transition · 2:00

Script: “Before we move on, say the idea in your own words. What comes before touching another person or their clothing? Carry your commitment into the next part of the day.”

Mastery evidence: Students can explain that another person's body requires that person's choice.

TUESDAY · WHEN A PRANK CROSSES THE LINE

Instructional target

K–2: Students will recognize that play must be safe and wanted by everyone involved.

3–5: Students will use a three-question diagnostic—Permission, Dignity, Safety—to determine when joking crosses a boundary.

6–8: Students will distinguish intent from impact and analyze how humiliation, exposure, and crowd behavior change the nature of an incident.

The source curriculum explicitly contrasts ordinary celebration/minor joking with clothing-pulling and public exposure, and emphasizes dignity, physical safety, and the body's threat response.

TUESDAY PRESENTER NOTES

01 · Welcome

Script: “Yesterday we learned who gets to decide what happens to a person's body. Today we apply that idea to joking and pranks. Our question is not ‘Did someone mean to be funny?’ Our question is ‘What actually happened to another person's choice, dignity, and safety?’”

02 · Regulate

Script: “When a crowd laughs, our brains sometimes want to join before we have even decided what we think. Give yourself a moment to notice before reacting.”

K–2 language: “You never have to laugh just because somebody else is laughing.”

Look for: Students can pause without teacher prescribing a particular emotional response.

03 · Orient

Script: “We need something stronger than ‘I thought it was funny.’ We are going to use three questions.”

For 3–8, point to each word:

Permission: “Did the person clearly agree?” Dignity: “Did the action protect their privacy and humanity?” Safety: “Could this make their body or the environment unsafe?”

6–8 extension: “Bodies can respond to a sudden boundary violation with fight, flight, or freeze. Someone does not have to cry, yell, or fight back for the action to be serious.”

04 · Connect · Look

Script: “Classify the behavior, not the person. Do not call anyone a bully, predator, bad kid, or victim. Tell me which evidence changes your conclusion.”

3–5 misconception check: “If the person laughs after being embarrassed, does that automatically equal permission?” Expected: No.

6–8 misconception check: “If nobody was punched, can dignity or safety still be violated?” Expected: Yes.

05 · Connect · Share

Script: “Ten seconds to think. You may analyze the fictional scenario only. Partner A, then Partner B. Passing is fine.”

Look for: Students recognize stop language, withdrawal, exposure, loss of privacy, or lack of permission as evidence.

06 · Practice

K–2 teacher response: “If Sam said stop, we don't need to decide whether Jordan meant to be funny. The action stops.”

3–5 facilitation: Have students show 1–3 fingers as the class checks Permission, Dignity, and Safety. Do not turn the scenario into a debate over whether Marcus “overreacted.”

6–8 facilitation: Ask for three immediate actions:

1. Stop the violation and protect privacy.

2. Remove the audience/phones and get adult support.

3. Check on the affected student privately.

Look for: Students move from judgment to action.

07 · Commit

Script: “A commitment should change a decision you might actually make today. Make it concrete.”

Strong examples include asking first, stopping when told, refusing to film, not feeding a crowd, or getting help.

08 · Transition

Script: “Say the three-question test with me once: Permission. Dignity. Safety. That is the tool. Carry it with you.”

Mastery evidence: Students can explain why an action can cross a boundary even when someone calls it a joke.

WEDNESDAY PRESENTER NOTES

01 · Welcome

Script: “The person causing harm is responsible for that choice. Bystanders are responsible for what they choose next. Today we are learning choices that can make a harmful moment smaller instead of bigger.”

This distinction is important: students should never leave believing a harmed student was unsafe because peers “failed” to rescue them.

02 · Regulate

Script: “When something happens fast, our job is not to become another unsafe person. Pause long enough to choose a move that helps.”

Say explicitly: “Helping does not mean grabbing, threatening, fighting, or putting yourself in danger.”

03 · Orient

Teacher modeling:

Direct: “Stop. Hands off.” Not: a speech, insult, threat, or argument.

Distract: “Bell's ringing. Clear the hallway.” The goal is to break momentum or audience attention.

Delegate: “I need an adult at the west restroom now. Someone is crossing a body boundary.” Specific information gets faster help.

Delay: “Are you okay? Do you want me to walk with you to an adult?” Do not demand details.

K–2: Teach the plain-language labels first; expose students to the D-word underneath but do not require memorization.

04 · Connect · Look

Script: “Look at actions, not facial expressions. Which students are feeding the crowd? Which students are reducing the audience, getting help, or supporting the person?”

6–8 extension: “A crowd can create a social reward—attention, laughter, video, status. Removing that reward can be an intervention.”

05 · Connect · Share

Script: “Not every student should use Direct every time. Safety matters. Sometimes Delegate is the smartest and strongest move.”

Misconception correction: Getting adult help for a bodily-safety concern is not “tattling.” Tattling seeks to get someone in trouble over a minor issue; safety reporting seeks protection or help.

06 · Practice

Critical direction: “No one acts out the boundary violation. One person reads the scenario. The other person rehearses only the intervention.”

K–2: Teacher points to the four visual cards and allows students to select rather than generate language independently.

3–5: Require students to explain why the D fits.

6–8: Require sequence thinking: “What would you do first? What would you do afterward?”

Look for: Safe, brief, behavior-focused language.

Do not accept: “You're disgusting.” “What's wrong with you?” “I'll beat you up.” “Everybody record this.”

Redirect to: “Stop. Hands off.” “Give them space.” “I need an adult here.”

07 · Commit

Script: “Pick the move you can realistically use. A strong commitment is not ‘I'll stop everything.’ It is ‘I can get an adult,’ ‘I can refuse to record,’ or ‘I can check on the person afterward.’”

08 · Transition

Script: “Intervening does not require being the loudest person in the hallway. A helpful action reduces harm.”

Mastery evidence: Students can name a safe bystander move and apply it to a scenario.

THURSDAY PRESENTER NOTES

01 · Welcome

Script: “This week we learned how to protect our own boundaries, recognize when a line is crossed, and act when we see harm. Today we answer the final question: What happens when I am the person who crossed the line?”

“Making a harmful choice does not have to become your identity. Accountability means dealing truthfully with the choice and changing what happens next.”

02 · Regulate

Script: “Repair is difficult when our brains are busy defending ourselves. Stop. Get enough control of your body to listen. Then tell the truth.”

Do not require breathing. Offer posture change, feet on floor, quiet pause, water, or another established regulation strategy.

03 · Orient

Script: “Real repair has four parts.”

STOP — “The harmful action ends.” OWN — “Name what you did without adding ‘but.’” REPAIR — “Follow the appropriate plan to address what can be addressed.” REBUILD — “Future behavior becomes evidence.”

Clarification: Repair is not always a conversation with the harmed person. The adult-supported repair may be a written reflection, restitution, changed routine, boundary plan, restorative conference when appropriate, or another action.

04 · Connect · Look

Script: “This is not a poster we hang because the words sound good. Each line describes something people should be able to experience here.”

Read each line.

After each, ask:

“What would we SEE if this were true?”

Examples:

- hands remaining off another person's clothing;

- phones staying away from someone's embarrassment;

- students getting adults rather than gathering an audience;

- changed behavior after correction.

05 · Connect · Share

Script: “Think about the difference between words and evidence. You may use a fictional situation.”

K–2: steer from “say sorry” toward actions. 3–5: look for honesty + change. 6–8: look for behavior, not emotional performance.

06 · Practice

Script: “An excuse tries to reduce responsibility. Accountability accurately names responsibility.”

Write or display:

I did ____. It affected ____. I will repair by ____. Next time I will ____.

K–2 abbreviated frame:

I ____. That was not okay. Next time I will ____.

Important: Students do not write apologies to actual peers during this universal lesson.

6–8 challenge: Remove these four accountability-killers:

- “but”

- “everybody”

- “they made me”

- “it wasn't that serious”

The goal is not perfect wording. The goal is accurate ownership.

07 · Commit

Script: “Choose one covenant line that you want your behavior to prove today.”

Students may keep the commitment private.

If using a physical class covenant, students may initial it later during the day. Do not spend the full instructional block forming a line to sign a poster.

08 · Transition

Script: “This week's learning can fit into four sentences:

My body belongs to me. Your body belongs to you. Our choices can protect each other. When we cause harm, we repair it.

Carry that standard into the rest of the day.”

Mastery evidence: Students distinguish apology language from observable accountability and repair.

Permission · Dignity · Safety Decision Card

3. SAFETY Does the action keep their body and environment safe?

THE 4 Ds

DIRECT — Say what needs to stop. DISTRACT — Break the momentum. DELEGATE — Get adult help. DELAY — Check in afterward.

THE BOUNDARY ANALYSIS CARD

Permission What observable evidence shows agreement?

Dignity Is someone's body, clothing, privacy, or embarrassment becoming entertainment?

Safety Could this create physical risk or activate a threat response?

Crowd Are observers increasing or reducing the harm?

ACCOUNTABILITY TEST

Before calling it repair, ask:

Did I name my action accurately? Did I remove excuses? Did I complete the repair expected of me? Can my future behavior show change?

CLASSROOM COVENANT

OUR BODILY AUTONOMY & DIGNITY COVENANT

WE OWN OUR BODIES. We respect another person's right to control their body, clothing, and personal space.

WE PROTECT PRIVACY. We do not turn someone's embarrassment, exposure, or distress into entertainment.

WE ACT WHEN HELP IS NEEDED. We use safe bystander choices and involve adults when needed.

WE REPAIR HARM. When we cross a boundary, we stop, own our action, complete appropriate repair, and change the pattern.

This is what belonging requires: your place in our community remains intact, and our standards remain intact too.
