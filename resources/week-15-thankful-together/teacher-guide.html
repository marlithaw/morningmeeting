Week hubTeacher guide

Teach Thankful Together

Monday through Thursday, each grade band practices one part of gratitude: noticing the good, saying a specific thank-you (what you did and how it helped), showing gratitude through action, and thanking the school community. The language matches the gratitude lessons homerooms receive this month from the counseling team, so students hear the same words from every adult: notice it, name it, show it. Friday’s Morning Spark (Friday Wonder) carries the same theme, followed by Friday Cypher.

Timing: Welcome ready screen; Regulate 2, Orient 2, Connect 3 (Look 2 and Share 1), Practice 3, Commit 3, Transition 2 minutes.

Respect and privacy, every day: gratitude is personal, and students choose what they share. Students may name a thing, a place, a pet, an activity, or a person; no one has to name family or explain what they have or do not have. Never ask what students are getting, eating, or doing over a holiday. If a share touches something hard, say: “Thank you for trusting us. You can talk with me after,” and follow up privately. Welcome responses by writing, drawing, speaking, pointing, signing, or a student’s usual communication tool, and in any language.

Video clips: each “Watch 60 seconds” button opens the YouTube video with captions on and stops at one minute. If a video will not load, read the key message aloud a second time and continue.

Participation moves for every lesson

Equity sticks (cold call): keep one stick per student in a “not yet called” cup. Always give think time first, then say, “Everyone has an answer. I will pull a stick.” Pull, read the name, ask the question, and move the stick to the “called” cup. If a student needs a moment, say “I will come back to you,” call one more student, and return. Accept pointing, signing, or a device response. The scripts name exactly when to pull a stick.

Turn and talk: partners A and B. “Partner A answers first. Partner B, listen so you can repeat it.” After 30 seconds: “Switch.” Then cold call: “Tell us what your partner said.” K–2 partners sit knee to knee for 10 to 15 seconds.

Talk moves: “Say more.” “Who can add on?” “Do you agree or disagree? Why?” “Say it in your own words.” “What in the video or slide makes you think that?”

LiveSchool points: award during the work, name the Safe, Respectful, or Responsible behavior out loud, then tap. Each lesson script includes the exact words. This week, connect points to gratitude students can see: a specific thank-you, holding a door, helping clean up without being asked, including a classmate, and taking care of shared materials. About four positives for every correction. Never award points for being quiet or for finishing first.

Behavior supports: keep the lesson moving. Give one short reminder that names the expectation, then tally it and keep teaching. Each day’s Transition note has the exact words for the most likely disruption. Gratitude shares can bring out silliness or put-downs (“I’m thankful for nothing”); respond calmly, keep the frame, and move to the next student. Safety comes first: any unsafe behavior gets immediate adult support.

Monday November 16 · Grades K–2 · Indiana SEL 1B Self-Awareness · 5A Connection

Notice the Good

Target: I can notice something good and say what it is.

Key message: Being thankful starts with noticing. A sunny sky, a friend who shares, a story we love, a warm coat. When we look for the good, we find more of it.

Prepare: Good Things I See page from the K–2 toolkit (one per student); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: Thankful (The Juicebox Jukebox).

Evidence: The child colors at least two good things and draws or tells one more good thing they noticed.

Differentiate: Kindergarten: point to each picture and name it with an adult. Grade 1: say one sentence about a good thing. Grade 2: write one good thing and who or what made it happen.

Answer notes: Any good thing counts: a friend, a book, recess, a song, a teacher, sunshine, a snack, a pet. Drawings of people, places, or things are all correct.

Take home: Good Things. Family chat: What is one good thing you noticed today?

Notice the Good

Target: I can explain how noticing the good changes my attention, and practice it on purpose.

Key message: The brain is wired to notice threats and problems first. It is called the negativity bias. Gratitude is a skill that trains attention to also catch what is going right. It does not erase real problems. It adds what is good to the picture.

Prepare: Train Your Attention page from the 6–8 toolkit (one per student); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: The Science of Gratitude (Tremendousness).

Evidence: The student rewrites a one-sided day into a full picture that keeps the problem and adds specific good moments, then logs three good things with a reason.

Differentiate: Grade 6: use the chips to log good things. Grades 7–8: explain the negativity bias in your own words and why it helped humans survive. Accept oral responses.

Answer notes: The second student noticed help from a friend and a good lunch; the first noticed only problems. The second student is not ignoring problems: the hard quiz is still named. A strong rewrite keeps the problem and adds two specific good moments.

Take home: The Full Picture. Family chat: What is something good in your life that is easy to take for granted?

Target: I can give a specific thank-you that names what someone did and how it helped me.

Key message: “Thanks” is polite. A specific thank-you is powerful. It has two parts: what the person did, and how it helped. “Thank you for explaining the steps. It helped me finish my math.” The person learns their kindness mattered.

Prepare: Level Up Your Thank-You page from the 3–5 toolkit (one per student); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: Kid President’s 25 Reasons To Be Thankful! (SoulPancake).

Differentiate: Grade 3: upgrade two using the chips. Grades 4–5: upgrade all three and add how the helper might feel. Students may draw and dictate.

Answer notes: Strong upgrades name an action and an impact, for example: “Thank you for lending me your eraser. It helped me fix my answer.” “Thank you for saving me a seat. I didn’t have to eat alone.” “Thank you for explaining the directions. Now I know what to do.”

Take home: Specific Thanks. Family chat: Who is someone we could thank, and what exactly would we say?

Say Thank You Like You Mean It

Target: I can express gratitude specifically and sincerely, naming the action, the impact, and what it meant to me.

Key message: Research on gratitude finds that people underestimate how much a thank-you means to the person receiving it, and overestimate how awkward it will feel to say. A sincere thank-you is specific: what they did, what it changed, and what it meant to you.

Prepare: Say It Like You Mean It page from the 6–8 toolkit (one per student); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: An Experiment in Gratitude | The Science of Happiness (SoulPancake).

Evidence: The student ranks thank-yous by specificity and drafts a sincere thank-you with an action, an impact, and personal meaning.

Differentiate: Grade 6: draft using the chips. Grades 7–8: explain why people underestimate how much a thank-you means. Write in any language.

Answer notes: Weakest: “thx.” Middle: “Thanks for helping me.” Sincere: “Thank you for staying late to help me with free throws. I made both at the game, and it meant a lot that you believed I could.” Any draft with an action, an impact, and a meaning is strong.

Take home: Say It Out Loud. Family chat: Who has made a difference for you that you have never told?

Open the 6–8 deck · Open the worksheet

Welcome · ready screen

Say: “Good morning. “Thanks” is good. A thank-you that names what someone did and how it helped is even better. Today we practice saying it like we mean it. Today’s focus is Say Thank You Like You Mean It.” Use the usual greeting. The recurring First 5 video may play before the timed meeting. Ground rule for today: we thank people for what they did, never for how they look or what they have.

Say: “Let’s get our bodies ready to learn together. Breathe in slowly. Breathe out slowly.” Model three comfortable breaths. Students may breathe, look at the slide, or rest their hands; no one has to close their eyes. Then read the third card aloud: “Kind words ready.” Keep individual supports such as fidgets, seats, and visual schedules available as usual.

Ask students to read the goal with you. Check for understanding with an equity stick: “Xavier, which picture shows something you could do today?” Accept pointing. Say: “Yes. We will practice that right now.”

Before the clip, say: “Watch for this question: What happens when people share their gratitude out loud?” Press “Watch 60 seconds.” The clip opens with captions on and stops at 1:00 (An Experiment in Gratitude | The Science of Happiness, SoulPancake). If the video does not load, read the message on the slide a second time and go straight to the question. After the clip, give five seconds of think time, then pull an equity stick: “Destiny, what happens when people share their gratitude out loud?” Talk move: “Can you say more?” Then: “Who can add on to that?”

Connect · Share · 1:00

Read the situation aloud: “Jordan’s coach stayed late three times to help him with free throws. Jordan wants to thank her but says, “It’s awkward. She knows I’m grateful.”” Then read each question: Does the coach really know? What could she be missing? What would a sincere, specific thank-you from Jordan sound like? Give ten seconds of silent think time. Turn and talk: “Partner A answers the first question. Partner B answers the second. Go.” After 30 seconds, say “Switch.” Equity stick: pull two sticks. Ask Jordan, “What did your partner say?” Ask Camila, “Do you agree or want to add on? Say more.” Close with the slide line: “A thank-you means more to them than you think, and costs you less than you fear.” Strong response: A strong answer says the coach cannot know what the extra practice changed for Jordan unless he tells her. A sincere thank-you names the action (staying late), the impact (his free throws improved), and what it meant (someone believed in him).

Commit · 3:00

Model: “Someone I have never fully thanked is my older cousin, and I would tell them, “Thank you for walking me to school in sixth grade. It made a new school feel less scary.”” Give 90 seconds to finish the frame by drawing, writing, or telling a partner. Commitments stay private unless a student chooses to share. Pull one equity stick for a volunteer share; a student may pass by saying “I am still thinking.” Students fold the page and put it in their take-home folder.

Transition · 2:00

Say: “Awkward lasts a second. A sincere thank-you can stay with someone for years.” Name the next class task and where to start. Use your established release cue. Behavior support: If students laugh during the video or the shares, name it lightly: “It can feel awkward. That’s the point of practicing.” Keep the next cold call low risk (rate the examples) before asking anyone to share their own thank-you.

Gratitude in Action

Target: I can turn gratitude into action and explain how it strengthens our community.

Key message: Gratitude in action means paying it forward: someone helped you, so you help someone else. Respecting shared spaces, backing up a classmate, and including someone new are actions that say “I value this place and these people.” Gratitude spreads when it is shown.

Prepare: Pay It Forward page from the 6–8 toolkit (one per student); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: Kids Talk Gratitude (Workhuman).

Evidence: The student traces a gratitude chain, picks a specific action, and explains how visible actions spread in a community.

Differentiate: Grade 6: choose an action from the chips. Grades 7–8: explain one way gratitude in action can change a class or team culture. Accept oral responses.

Answer notes: Paying it forward: share notes, explain the directions, offer to sit together during catch-up time. Actions spread because people see them and copy them; words alone can be missed. Any chain with a real helper, a specific action, and a person to help is strong.

Take home: Pay It Forward. Family chat: Tell about a time someone helped you and you passed it on.

Thankful for Our Community

Target: I can write a sincere thank-you note to a member of our school community and explain why gratitude strengthens a community.

Key message: A school is a community. Bus drivers, the cafeteria team, custodians, the front office, the nurse, teachers, and classmates all keep it running. Communities get stronger when people feel seen. A sincere thank-you note is proof someone noticed.

Prepare: Community Thank-You Note page from the 6–8 toolkit (one per student); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: What Teens Are Thankful For (Greater Good Science Center).
