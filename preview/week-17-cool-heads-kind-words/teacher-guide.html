Week hubTeacher guide

Teach Cool Heads, Kind Words

Monday through Thursday, each grade band practices one step of handling a strong feeling: notice the spark (body warning signs), cool down first (a tool before an action), talk it out (an I-statement with no put-downs or cursing), and safe hands, strong choices (walk away, get an adult, do not hype a fight). The same four words run all week in every band, so students hear one message from every adult: notice it, cool it, talk it out. Friday’s Morning Spark (Friday Wonder) carries the same theme, followed by Friday Cypher.

Timing: Welcome ready screen; Regulate 2, Orient 2, Connect 3 (Look 2 and Share 1), Practice 3, Commit 3, Transition 2 minutes.

Respect and privacy, every day: students talk about the skill, not about a real conflict with a real classmate. Scenarios use made-up names; no one retells a fight, names someone they are mad at, or explains what happens at home. Anger is a normal feeling, and no student is shamed for having it. If a share points to a current conflict or a safety concern, say: “Thank you for telling me. Let’s talk after,” and follow the school’s support and reporting steps privately. Welcome responses by writing, drawing, speaking, pointing, signing, or a student’s usual communication tool, and in any language.

Video clips: each “Watch 60 seconds” button opens the YouTube video with captions on and stops at one minute. If a video will not load, read the key message aloud a second time and continue.

Participation moves for every lesson

Equity sticks (cold call): keep one stick per student in a “not yet called” cup. Always give think time first, then say, “Everyone has an answer. I will pull a stick.” Pull, read the name, ask the question, and move the stick to the “called” cup. If a student needs a moment, say “I will come back to you,” call one more student, and return. Accept pointing, signing, or a device response. The scripts name exactly when to pull a stick.

Turn and talk: partners A and B. “Partner A answers first. Partner B, listen so you can repeat it.” After 30 seconds: “Switch.” Then cold call: “Tell us what your partner said.” K–2 partners sit knee to knee for 10 to 15 seconds.

Talk moves: “Say more.” “Who can add on?” “Do you agree or disagree? Why?” “Say it in your own words.” “What in the video or slide makes you think that?”

LiveSchool points: award during the work, name the Safe, Respectful, or Responsible behavior out loud, then tap. Each lesson script includes the exact words. This week, connect points to calm students can see: taking a breath before answering, using a calm voice in a disagreement, an I-statement, walking away from a conflict, and asking an adult for help. About four positives for every correction. Never award points for being quiet or for finishing first.

Behavior supports: keep the lesson moving. Give one short reminder that names the expectation, then tally it and keep teaching. Each day’s Transition note has the exact words for the most likely disruption. Acting out anger can get silly or loud; model the calm version once, then call on a student who is using it. Keep every role-play seated and hands-free. Safety comes first: any unsafe behavior gets immediate adult support through the school’s usual steps.

Monday December 7 · Grades K–2 · Indiana SEL 1A Self-Awareness · 2A Self-Management

Notice the Spark

Target: I can notice when my body feels mad.

Key message: Everybody feels mad sometimes. Mad can feel like a hot face, tight fists, a fast heart, or a loud voice. When we notice our body, we can stop and choose what to do.

Prepare: My Mad Body page from the K–2 toolkit (one per student); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: What Do You Do With the Mad That You Feel? (Daniel Tiger).

Evidence: The child colors at least one body part that feels mad and circles a face, then says or points to “I feel mad.”

Differentiate: Kindergarten: point to the body pictures while an adult names them. Grade 1: say “I feel mad when ___.” Grade 2: write one warning sign next to the drawing.

Answer notes: Any body part a child colors is correct: face, hands, chest, tummy, head, voice. Any face the child says looks mad is correct.

Take home: My Mad Body. Family chat: Where do you feel mad in your body?

Notice the Spark

Target: I can name the warning signs my body sends when anger starts, before it takes over.

Key message: Anger is a normal feeling, and it gives us warning signs: a hot face, a tight jaw, clenched fists, a racing heart, a loud voice. Anger grows fast, like a spark that turns into a fire. If we catch the spark early, we stay in charge of what we do next.

Prepare: My Warning Signs page from the 3–5 toolkit (one per student); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: Why Do We Lose Control of Our Emotions? (Kids Want to Know).

Evidence: The student circles at least two warning signs, rates how fast anger grows, and names a first sign.

Differentiate: Grade 3: circle signs using the chips. Grades 4–5: rank your signs from first to last and explain which one is easiest to catch. Students may draw and dictate.

Answer notes: Malik’s signs: tight jaw, hot face, yelling. He could catch it at the tight jaw or hot face, before the yelling. Any warning signs a student circles for themselves are correct.

Take home: My Warning Signs. Family chat: What is your first sign that you are getting mad?

Monday December 7 · Grades 6–8 · Indiana SEL 1A Self-Awareness · 2A Self-Management

Notice the Spark

Target: I can explain what happens in my brain when I “flip my lid” and name my early warning signs.

Key message: When anger spikes, the thinking part of the brain goes offline and the alarm part takes over. That is called flipping your lid. It happens to everyone. The goal is not to never get angry. It is to notice the early signs and get your lid back down before you say or do something you cannot take back.

Prepare: Know Your Lid page from the 6–8 toolkit (one per student); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: Dan Siegel’s Hand Model of the Brain, Flip Your Lid (Mo Mindful).

Evidence: The student labels the alarm brain and thinking brain on the hand model and lists three personal early warning signs.

Differentiate: Grade 6: use the chips to list signs. Grades 7–8: explain in one sentence why the alarm brain helped humans survive and why it misfires at school. Accept oral responses.

Answer notes: Thumb: the alarm, feeling brain. Fingers: the thinking brain. Flipping the lid means the thinking brain goes offline. Jordan’s early signs: pounding heart, burning face, standing up fast. Any three personal signs are correct.

Take home: Know Your Lid. Family chat: What helps you get your lid back down?

Cool Down First

Target: I can use a cool-down tool when I feel mad.

Key message: When you feel so mad, cool down first. Take a deep breath and count to four. Squeeze your hands tight and let them go. Ask for the calm corner. A calm body helps you make a good choice.

Prepare: My Cool-Down Tools page from the K–2 toolkit (one per student); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: Take a Deep Breath and Count to 4 (Daniel Tiger).

Differentiate: Kindergarten: point to each tool while an adult models it. Grade 1: show the tool with your body and say its name. Grade 2: write the name of your favorite tool.

Answer notes: All three tools are correct: belly breathing, squeezing and letting go, the calm corner. Any tool the child draws or names that is safe is correct.

Take home: Cool-Down Tools. Family chat: What helps you calm down when you feel mad?

Cool Down First

Target: I can use a cool-down tool before I react, and build my own cool-down plan.

Key message: A cool-down tool turns the heat down so the thinking brain can come back. Tools work best when you practice them while you are calm, like a fire drill. Breathing, counting, squeezing a fist and letting go, getting water, or asking for a short break all count.

Prepare: My Cool-Down Plan page from the 3–5 toolkit (one per student); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: Melting Exercise - Learn To Destress (GoNoodle).

Evidence: The student practices two tools and writes a four-step plan that names a specific tool and a calm next step.

Differentiate: Grade 3: complete the plan using the chips. Grades 4–5: add which tool works best in class, at recess, and at home, and why. Students may draw and dictate.

Answer notes: Seat tools for Ava: breathing, counting, squeezing and letting go. Calm next steps: ask the group to rebuild, say how she feels, tell the teacher. A strong plan: notice tight fists, rainbow breath three times, ask for water, come back ready to rebuild.

Take home: Cool-Down Plan. Family chat: What do grown-ups at home do to cool down?

Safe Hands, Strong Choices

Target: I can explain why walking away from a fight is the strong choice and refuse to hype, film, or join one.

Key message: A fight can feel like a test you have to pass. It is not. Fights end with someone hurt, a consequence, and a problem that is still not solved. Walking away takes more strength than swinging. Hyping, filming, or posting a fight makes you part of it. The strong move is to step back, get an adult, and help a friend leave.

Prepare: The Strong Move page from the 6–8 toolkit (one per student); pencils and crayons; equity sticks; LiveSchool open; Show-Me cards from the toolkit. Video: It’s ok to walk away from a fight! (Jeff Phillips).

Evidence: The student weighs the costs of fighting against walking away, writes a personal exit line, and writes a plan that includes getting an adult and not hyping, filming, or posting.

Differentiate: Grade 6: use the chips for the exit line and plan. Grades 7–8: explain how an audience, in person or online, raises the pressure in a conflict. Accept oral responses.

Answer notes: A fight can cost an injury, a consequence, a damaged friendship, and a video that does not go away, and the problem stays unsolved. Walking away may cost a few comments. The people filming and hyping add pressure and an audience. A strong plan: exit line, step back, get an adult, phone away.

Take home: The Strong Move. Family chat: When is walking away the strongest thing to do?
